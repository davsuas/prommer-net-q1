Bosses credit AI instead of employees, delaying promotions and raises

When companies made engineers footnote their work as "cowritten by an AI
assistant," something unexpected happened: people quietly stopped reaching for
the tools. Rather than have their best contributions diminished, engineers
went back to doing the work the slow way.

Thomas Prommer, a technology executive who advises companies on applied AI, has
watched the pattern play out across several engineering teams. The teams that
recovered, he says, did so by changing who gets the credit. Instead of tracking
how much AI touched a piece of work, they tracked outcomes, and the responsible
person owns the credit and the blame regardless of how much AI helped.

That reframing, Prommer argues, restored the incentive to use the tools well.
The goal was never to measure AI usage for its own sake; it was to ship better
work and let the evidence carry it.
